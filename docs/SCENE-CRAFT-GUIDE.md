# Imprompt Scene Craft Guide

## Two private prompts. One shared scene.

Each performer privately draws a **Stance** and a **Drive**. Those cards create starting conditions; they are not scripts, separate plots, or private win conditions.

> **Use the card to enter the scene. Use your partner to discover the scene.**

## Stance, Drive, and shared pattern

- **Stance:** your private point of view—how you interpret yourself, the other person, or the situation.
- **Drive:** your initial pressure—what you pursue, protect, avoid, discover, or repeatedly do.
- **Shared pattern:** the repeatable cause-and-effect behavior both performers discover together. Some improvisers call this the game of the scene.

A useful formula is:

> **Your Stance + Your Drive + Your partner’s offers = The emerging scene**

## How two Drives become one scene

Two Drives can combine in three common ways.

### They align

Both pressures point in a compatible direction. One player may want reassurance while the other wants to prove they are indispensable. The shared pattern might become an escalating exchange in which both offer reassurance mainly to receive more reassurance.

### They oppose

One pursuit creates resistance from the other. One player may want the other to stay while the other avoids discussing why they are leaving. The shared pattern becomes pursuit and evasion.

### One becomes dominant

One Drive may reveal the clearest repeatable behavior while the other becomes resistance, support, emotional subtext, or a reason to heighten it. Both Drives do not need equal attention.

## The Convergence Rule

> **Your cards start the scene; they do not own the scene. When a shared pattern emerges, support it—even when it changes, frustrates, or replaces your original Drive.**

The priority is:

> **Partner first → shared pattern second → private card third**

### Five-step method

1. Make one clear behavioral offer from the Drive.
2. Give it another opportunity when the scene naturally permits it.
3. Notice what your partner repeatedly does in response.
4. Heighten that shared cause-and-effect pattern.
5. Bend, reinterpret, or release the original card when forcing it would fight the scene.

## Secret does not mean cryptic

Your partner should not know the exact wording of your card, but they should be able to respond to the behavior it creates. Keep the prompt private; make its effect playable.

## A Drive is not a win condition

A performer does not succeed by completing the objective, forcing compliance, making the partner guess the card, or preserving the original plan at all costs. A failed objective can create a stronger comic pattern than a completed one.

The useful question is:

> **Did our private cards help us discover something repeatable together?**

## Postmortem questions

- What shared pattern emerged?
- Did the Drives align, oppose, or become unequal?
- What did you think your partner wanted?
- When did each card become observable?
- When did following the card help the scene?
- When would bending or releasing it have helped?
- What could have been repeated or heightened next?

## Coach guidance

Ask each player to make one clear offer from the Drive, then observe the response it produces. Pause when performers defend two unrelated plans and neither person’s behavior changes the other. Restart from the last moment where one choice caused a visible reaction, repeat that exchange, and heighten it.

- **Mirror exercises:** explore what happens when both performers bring the same kind of dramatic pressure through independently drawn cards.
- **Paired exercises:** explore how complementary functions feed, resist, ground, or heighten one another.

The coaching goal is not perfect execution of two assignments. It is convergence through listening.
